1.1

Introduction

Infrastructure investment has always been one of the key factors driving economic development. Infrastructure investment not only enhances economic efficiency and productivity but also ripples its effects on reducing economic inequality through the expansion of opportunities to markets and employment (Bado & Dunakhir, 2024). Depending on the type of infrastructure, the effects of these investments differ from one another, thus focusing on the infrastructure that yields the best possible result specific to a certain region relative to the others can help the economy prosper more efficiently.

According to Nisa & Khalid (2024) developing countries, such as the Philippines, tend to significantly benefit more from the economic growth brought by infrastructure investment compared to developed ones. This view, however, is still not well studied at the sub-national level in most of the developing countries. Many of these countries such as the Philippines, India, Indonesia, Myanmar, Bangladesh, Haiti, Dominica, and South Sudan experience a more severe effect from the external forces than the developed ones. Adding external factors such as corruption, risk of natural disaster, global inflation, and macroeconomic shocks makes assessing the benefits of infrastructure investment more challenging.

For the Philippine context, every administration tends to place a focus on infrastructure development projects. The budget was spread across expressways, bridges, buildings, and transportation depending on the project that the administration aimed to achieve. However, some of these public investments are not effective in doing what they are intended to do. Critiques of these projects, such as Tiongco et al. (2026) state that the lack of transparency and the misuse of funds of the officials in charge distorts economic development and dilutes the delivery of the essential public service for the people. Ang (2023) which did a comprehensive report on Build, Better, More (BBM) also stated that the role of infrastructure on the development of the economy is significant, adding that it also impacts the social autonomy of the population.

The effect of these investments is not direct, and this can be seen through a multi-stage economic model. This model can be described as a three-step process: government funds are committed to a certain infrastructure contract, this leads to the building of the project, which then ripples to the economic development of the country, and finally reaches the point where a worker is hired somewhere in the regional economy. Spending must first be converted into verified physical assets before confirming that the asset can truly contribute to regional productive capacity and output and eventually translates it into labor demand. Each of these processes can fail in cases where: funds may be awarded but delayed in execution, completed infrastructure can fail to translate proportionally into regional output if consumptions or other demand-side factors are weak, and regional output can grow without generating a proportional increase in employment.

Jiang et al. (2017) concluded that the effect of new infrastructure spending on economic growth highly depends on the region. According to Duffy-Deno and Eberts (1991), the effect of newly built infrastructure to per capita personal income comes in two channels. The first one is the actual construction of public capital stock. The second one is infrastructure helping businesses produce more for free, leading to a boost in people's everyday life.

A study on local fiscal multiplier effect in the Philippine setting produced a significant result. Debuque-Gonzales (2020) concluded that a 1-peso rise in local government spending translates into a 1.2 peso rise in regional output. Debuque-Gonzales (2020) also added that spillover effects are far larger showing the important role of domestic trade and connectivity in stimulating regional economic activity. This connects to World Bank's Policy Research Working Paper 10954, which reports that public investment multipliers across panels of developing and emerging economies range from roughly 0.6 on impact to 1.6 in the longer run, confirming that infrastructure spending does translate into measurable output gains, even as the exact magnitude varies by country context and estimation method. Preña and Tuaño (2026) found through an input-output analysis that targeted infrastructure investment can yield a 1.27 percentage point higher GDP and generate over 2.2 million more jobs compared to business-as-usual spending, with effects that vary across sectors due to differing multiplier responses and interindustry linkages.

Efayena et al. (2025) suggested the use of Public-Private Partnership (PPP) in West African regions. The study concluded that the funding for the infrastructure should come from the internal budget to expand infrastructure while also maintaining the existing ones. The Build, Build, Build (BBB) program by the Duterte administration drew heavily on PPP arrangement, resulting in a surge of infrastructure within a short span of time.

A diverse amount of literature points to the effect of infrastructure investment on economic growth and employment. Studies such as Jiang et al. (2017) and Nisa & Khalid (2024) point out that the effects are depending on the region. Jiang et al. (2017) used three stage least squares (3SLS) to study the multimodal effect in China, which resulted in the eastern region having the strongest direct growth effect, however, businesses increasingly relocated toward the central and western regions as connectivity improved, drawn by lower labor costs. While Duffy-Deno and Eberts (1991), Debuque-Gonzales (2020), Preña and Tuaño (2026) focused on a single region and still found a significant impact in economic growth. For the Philippine context, studies such as Preña and Tuaño (2026) and Tiongco et al. (2026) tackle the transparency and minimal impact of infrastructure to growth and concluded with several factors that might hinder the direct effect.

Existing literature in the Philippine setting, such as Debuque-Gonzales (2020), establishes that infrastructure investment affects regional economic growth, but leaves a specific gap this study addresses directly. Debuque-Gonzales (2020) studies local government spending's effect on regional output, but does not extend to employment, nor does it verify whether spending actually converts into completed physical infrastructure before affecting output. This study addresses both gaps by examining national government infrastructure investment specifically, and by modeling the transmission from investment to verified physical output before assessing its effect on regional output and ultimately, employment.

The paper's goal is to assess the impact of infrastructure investment, sourced from DPWH Transparency Portal, which is a transparency website that mirrors DPWH's own internal database, on the regional employment level. The method the study will use is the simultaneous equations model (SEM), with three stage least squares (3SLS) as the estimation technique. The study will conduct a comprehensive analysis of whether infrastructure investment translates into employment through each stage of this transmission process.

1.2

Theoretical Framework

This study is anchored on Keynesian Multiplier Theory which is developed by Keynes (1936) in "The General Theory of Employment, Interest, and Money". Building on the employment multiplier mechanism that was first formalized by Kahn (1931) in "The Relation of Home Investment to Unemployment". This theory explains the relationship between the government public spending on infrastructure and its effect on employment in the Philippines.

The center for this framework is Keynes (1936) Principle of Effective Demand, which holds that the level of outputs and employment in an economy is determined by the level of aggregate demand, rather than just adjusting automatically toward employment like what the classical economic theory assumed. Classical theory assumed that wages and prices would flexibly adjust to clear the labor market at full employment; however, Keynes argued that this adjustment does not happen immediately, as economies often settle the equilibrium with involuntary unemployment where such cases like workers are willing but cannot find work despite being available at the present wage that is being offered. In this study, the government expenditure acts as an exogenous injection into the circular flow of income. It acts as a policy that is introduced from outside the system rather than just a variable that is passively determined by the existing income or consumption. When this exogenous injection raises the aggregate demand, firms respond in return by expanding production and in this process, eventually hiring additional labor in order to meet the higher demand.

Kahn (1931) further extended this specifically to employment, he identified the two components of total employment effect that is generated by an investment. The first is primary employment, this includes the labor that is directly affected by the investment such as workers that are employed in construction of an infrastructure project. The second is identified as derived, or secondary employment. This is the additional employment that was generated in other jobs due to the income earned by primary workers being spent and re-spent on goods and services, this situation then creates more labor demand in sectors beyond the original investment. Kahn shows that the total employment effect is the sum of these two components and is necessarily larger than the primary employment created by the investment alone. Keynes (1936) generalized this relationship in chapter 10 of the General Theory as the investment multiplier:

ΔY = k · ΔI,   where   k = 1 / (1 − MPC)

Where ΔY represents the resulting change in aggregate income, ΔI represents the initial injection of investment spending or government spending. MPC on the other hand is the Marginal Propensity to Consume, while k is the multiplier, which is the factor by which an initial injection of spending is amplified as it circulates through the economy. Based on Kahn's original employment terms, the relationship can be shown as:

Total Employment = Primary Employment × Employment Multiplier

The degree of this multiplier depends on the extent on which income generated by the initial investment continues to circulate in the regional economy, rather than leaking out through savings, taxes, or buying the goods outside the region. Regions that have greater capacity absorb more labor demand and are theoretically expected to convert a given demand injection into new employment more readily than regions operating close to full employment within the labor segments, where the same increase in demand is more likely going to spread into wage growth rather than just new job creation.

Applying this theory in the study, the Department of Public Works and Highway (DPWH) awarded infrastructure spending represents the exogenous investment injection which is described by Keynes (1936) and Kahn (1931) in which this is the public funds that left government budget and circulated as payments for contractors, laborers, and material suppliers. Similar to Kahn's framework, this spending is expected to first generate primary employment, captured in this study through measure of physical infrastructure output, which represents the portion of awarded spending that has been verified as genuine, and physically realized construction activity against those of spending that remains unspent and/or delayed. This primary employment eventually circulates the income through the regional economy via a multiplier process described by the formula by generating secondary/derived employment in sectors beyond the direct construction activity. The overall regional employment level, which is measured through the Philippine Statistics Authority's Labor Force Survey captures both the primary work created directly by DPWH awarded infrastructure investment and derived/secondary employment that is generated through multiplier effect.

This theoretical grounding provides the basis for treating physical infrastructure as a necessary intervening mechanism between infrastructure spending and employment, rather than just a repeated step in the analysis. Kahn (1931) and Keynes (1936) locate the origin of the multiplier process specifically in the act of employing labor. An investment creates primary employment, and eventually, the money paid through wages for the primary employees circulates through the regional economy by spending the money earned through buying goods or services. Awarded funds of the government that do not show any verified construction activity shows an incomplete injection and should be expected not to produce the same degree of effect for primary employment increase and derived employment compared to the spending that has physically been realized. This directly shows the empirical framework of the study, in which the awarded or injected exogenous is first examined in relation to a measure of physical infrastructure output, and estimate its effect on regional employment level.

1.3

Conceptual Framework

The study utilizes a Simultaneous Equation Model (SEM) using the estimation technique Three Stage Least Squares (3SLS) to map out the sequential transmission of infrastructure spending toward employment. This model is used to assess whether DPWH infrastructure investment reflects regional growth and employment generation at the sub-national level. The study builds its process in three main steps: (1) ensure the correct infrastructure investment specification, defining how spending, physical output, and the instrument are measured. (2) Incorporate these variables into a Simultaneous Equation Model (SEM) to assess how investment flows into regional output, and lastly, (3) extend the model to estimate the resulting effect on regional employment.

DPWH Investment→Physical Output→Regional GDP→Employment

A simultaneous equations approach is used to check whether the disturbances across the three equations are correlated. A single OLS regression would produce a result but would oversee the correlation between variables. It would also be unable to separate the true effect of investment from contamination caused by unobserved regional factors, which can produce biased estimates. To address this, the study uses instrumental variable estimation using the regional share of awarded infrastructure value funded through Outside Infrastructure channels (DepEd's Basic Educational Facilities Fund (BEFF), DOH's Health Facilities Enhancement Program (HFEP), and Unprogrammed Appropriations) relative to standard Regular Infra General Appropriations Act (GAA) funding.

If the equations errors are correlated, 3SLS exploits that information and gives a more efficient, jointly estimated result. The equations below specify the model in full:

Physical Output = f(Investmentt, Investmentt−1) + Population + Disaster frequency
RGDP = f(Physical Output) + Private Consumption
Employment = f(RGDP) + Labor Force Participation Rate

Physical output (PHYOUT) is presented as a function of Investment (INVOUT), which is a monetary value of awardAmount that can be described as the real, deflated peso value of DPWH contracts. Investment is translated into physical output across five categories: (1) Bridges, (2) Buildings and Facilities, (3) Flood Control and Drainage, (4) Roads, and (5) Water Provision and Storage. The primary model uses the combined count of completed projects sorted per category, per region, and per year. The result will also be analyzed separately, this helps us identify which of the infrastructure categories yields the strongest employment effect.

Population (POP) and disaster frequency (DISFRE) serve as control variables for the first equation. POP is used due to the reason that more populous regions require a larger investment in infrastructure independent of the monetary value of that region. DISFRE accounts for the environmental factors of the economy. Some funded infrastructure projects, especially in the Philippines, delays its construction regardless of the amount committed due to the country's high exposure to typhoons and calamity events. The second equation defines Regional Gross Domestic Product (RGDP) as a function of Physical Output and Private Consumption (PRIVCON). The reasoning behind this equation is rooted in how the national income was computed. Where GDP is the sum of Consumption (C), Investment (I), and Government Spending (G). If we want to assess how investment and government spending affect the regional economy, private consumption must be controlled as well. Not adding consumption could wrongfully give the credit of output growth driven household spending to investment instead. The last equation shows Employment (EMP) as a function of RGDP and Labor Force Participation Rate (LFPR). This is to separate the labor supply side of the model from the demand for labor that RGDP is meant to capture.

The process runs from DPWH infrastructure spending to Physical Infrastructure Output, to Regional GDP, and finally to the resulting employment effect. In the research paradigm, Investment is the independent variable, the Simultaneous Equations Model is the process, and Employment is the dependent variable.

1.4

Statement of the Problem

By employing a Simultaneous Equation Model (SEM) the study wants to assess the effect of five infrastructure investments (Bridges, Buildings and Facilities, Flood control and drainage, Roads, Water provision and storage) on employment. The study sought to answer the following specific questions:

1

Does infrastructure investment have a significant effect on employment?

2

What are the categories that yield the most significant benefits at the regional level? And what regions benefit more from these infrastructure investments?

3

Does the effect of infrastructure investment on physical output occur within the same year, or does it manifest over a period of time?

1.5

Hypotheses

This study seeks to validate the following null hypothesis:

1

The infrastructure investment does not have a significant effect on employment generation.

2

There is no significant difference on infrastructure category's effect on employment.

3

There is no significant difference on the benefits of infrastructure investment on employment by regions in the Philippines.

4

There is no significant difference between the same-year and lagged effect of infrastructure investment on physical output.

1.6

Scope and Limitations of the Study

The study covers 16 out of 18 regions in the Philippines. BARMM was excluded due to insufficient data. The provinces comprising Negros Island Region (NIR) were reallocated to their previous regions, as NIR is still a newly established region: Negros Occidental will be added to its previous region, Western Visayas, while Negros Oriental and Siquijor will be added to Central Visayas. This was done to ensure that the data were consistent across the panel. The collected DPWH project records are available up to 2025. However, the researchers delimited the study to the period 2016 to 2024, as other variables were not yet fully available for 2025 at the time of data collection.

1.7

Significance of the Study

The significance of this research lies within the real-life experiences of the people. To empirically validate the socio-economic returns of public infrastructure spending in the Philippines. Anomalies about the government spending are a hot topic, the Department of Public Works and Highways (DPWH) faces intense scrutiny regarding budget allocations, corruption and the efficacy of the "Build Better More (BBM)" Program. This study provides the necessary, data-driven audit of the relationship between public infrastructure investment and employment level.

For Policymakers

The findings of this paper provide a data driven framework for Department of Public Works and Highways (DPWH) and Department of Economy, Planning, and Development of the Philippines (DEPDev), formerly NEDA, in evaluating the efficiency of fiscal allocations. The researchers aim to identify whether past and current infrastructure spending budget leakages and anomalies have diluted the intended economic multiplier.

For Local Government Units (LGUs)

For regional and city planners, this study helps to highlight the importance of proper building of infrastructure in achieving economic decentralization. The use of each variable; roads, buildings, water sewage, etc., illustrates how local infrastructure serves as a catalyst for regional growth. The LGU can treat this study as a regional guide that also highlights the less perceived region/s.

For the Filipino Labor Force

The main significance of this study lies in its accountability to the Filipino people. Centuries have passed, yet the quality of public infrastructure hasn't really changed. The lack of quality infrastructure has hindered regional mobility and opportunities. This research seeks to determine if the taxes are going into tangible improvement in employment and poverty reduction.

1.8

Definition of Terms

Absorptive Capacity

A government agency's capacity to convert funds obligated for infrastructure projects into actual disbursed spending and completed output within the intended timeframe. In this study, limited absorptive capacity is discussed as a possible explanation for delays between infrastructure budget obligation and its eventual employment effects.

Build, Build, Build (BBB) Program

The premier infrastructure initiative of the Duterte administration aimed at revitalizing and modernizing Philippine infrastructure from 2017-2022.

Build Better More (BBM) Program

Spearheaded during Marcos Jr.'s regime, the program serves as the continuation to the BBB Program aimed particularly in infrastructure projects.

Department of Public Works and Highways (DPWH)

The national government agency responsible for the planning, design, construction, and maintenance of the Philippines' public infrastructure network, and the source agency for the project records this study draws on through DPWH Transparency portal.

Employment Level

This study measures employment level as the total number of working persons reported for each of the 16 Philippine regions for each year from 2016 to 2024.

Jobless Growth

The event of economic expansion where rising output and GDP are not met with a proportional increase in high-quality employment or poverty reduction, often associated with structural shifts into service-based rather than labor-intensive industrial sectors.

Public Infrastructure Investment

Government expenditure directed toward the construction, rehabilitation, or development of capital assets such as roads, bridges, flood control systems, and other public works intended to stimulate economic activity.

Right of Way (ROW) Acquisition

The legal and administrative process wherein the government secures land or property needed for public infrastructure construction.

Underemployment

A labor market condition wherein employed individuals are working fewer hours than they desire or are engaged in work that does not fully use their skills or qualifications. Cited as a persistent structural feature of the Philippine labor market even during periods of employment growth.
