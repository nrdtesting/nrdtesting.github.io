2.1

Public Infrastructure Investment as an Exogenous Demand Injection

Public Infrastructure Investment is widely recognized in macroeconomic literature as a primary option for fiscal policy stimulus and an exogenous demand shock, capable of stimulating short-run aggregate demand and also capable of expanding long-term productive capacity and total factor productivity (Ramey, 2020). To empirically isolate the demand injection mechanism of public infrastructure investment, Abiad, Furceri, and Topalova (2016), examine the effect of public investment in 17 Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development economies from 1985 to 2013. In their study, to overcome the problems of fiscal foresight, where businesses and households anticipate government spending plans in advance and change their behavior in response before the money is spent, the study used local projection methods based on public investment forecast errors. In this study, they defined these forecast errors as the unanticipated difference between actual government spending and the public economic forecast from the OECD. The study's findings revealed that unexpected public investment shocks of 1 percent of GDP increase real GDP by 0.4 percent in the same year and by 1.4 percent after four years. Moreover, this spending reduces the unemployment rate by 0.11 percentage in the short term and by 0.35 percentage points over the midterm, while encouraging private businesses to invest alongside the government. Public Infrastructure Investment is said as economic stimulus as it is strongest during periods of economic recessions, where multipliers reach 1.5 up to 2.0 due to unused labor and factories absorbs the government demand without causing inflation or interest hikes.

Extending this empirical analysis to developing countries, Adarov, Clements and Jalles (2026) evaluated a broad panel of 129 Emerging Markets and Developing Economies from 1980 to 2019. Utilizing local projections alongside statistical methods like two-stage least squares (2SLS) and the Generalized Method of Moments, also known as GMM, in which they isolated government spending shocks by tracking the country specific deviation in cyclically adjusted real government investment. The study shows that, in their estimation, public investment shocks equal to a 1 percent of GDP increase real national output by 0.4 percent in the first year and 1.1 percent over the 5 years scope. While in countries with strong government efficiency and healthy fiscal budgets, this 5-year growth multiplier reached as high as 1.6 percent. According to Adarov et al. (2026), their study explained that in developing economies or nations where capital is scarce, building specific infrastructure directly eliminated major physical bottlenecks in power and transport costs, which crowds in private business investment by up to 2.2 percent, increases total factor productivity by 0.8 percent, and raises possible economic output by 1.1 percent after the five years.

In the Philippines, a major national infrastructure drive is the Build, Build, Build Program under Duterte's administration, which is also a program from its corresponding previous administration, and recently the present administration of Marcos Jr.'s Build Better More program is explicitly designed as one of the large-scale economic demand injections in the country. According to Navarro and Llanto (2014), in their historical research, they showed that Philippine public infrastructure spending averages 1.40 percent to 2.09 percent of GDP between 2008 and 2012, which leaves the country at the bottom ranks among ASEAN neighbors. In order for the country to break out of this low investment, national economic plans deliberately raised public capital expenditure, which targets 5-6 percent of GDP under the BBM program and up to 7.3% of GDP under the BBB program. An empirical study by Komatsuzaki (2019) using macroeconomic simulation confirmed that scaling up public investment in the Philippines creates a sustained national GDP expansion if the government delivers or establishes these infrastructure investments efficiently, as expanded physical capital stock permanently improves logistics efficiency and business productivity across transportation, energy, and trade subsectors in the long run.

Despite the clear economic link between public investment and growth, literature from development economics warns that financial spending alone is an incomplete measure of real regional job creation. Evaluating the success of infrastructure policy solely through government spending assumes that every peso disbursed instantly transforms into an operational basic asset. In reality, public infrastructure, from investment to physical output, faces multiyear construction delays and execution hurdles that separate monetary outlays from physical asset delivery. A study by Leeper et al. (2010) and Bouakez et al. (2017) generally shows that time to spend, as budget approval lags and time to build, as construction time, are separate from initial demand outlays and the long run productivity gains. In the Philippine context, Patalinghug (2020) reveals that the execution gap using the Data Envelopment Analysis framework, shows that the value of public investment, which is a certain worth of functional infrastructure stock, is delivered less effectively in the Philippines. The study explains that the nominal budget outlays are heavily dispersed by bureaucratic delays, procurement rigidities, right-of-way disputes, and administrative waste, which estimates how infrastructure creates regional jobs that requires looking beyond financial expenditure to examine these institutional bottlenecks and actual physical project completion (Patalinghug, 2020).

2.2

The Keynesian Multiplier and Kahn's Employment Mechanism

In order to fully grasp the effect of government expenditure in employment, this study recognizes and utilizes the Keynesian Multiplier Theory and Kahn's Employment Mechanism. This gives the study a more realistic and specific approach to modelling employment effects by treating government expenditure as an exogenous injection rather than the traditional Keynesian income-expenditure model that solely relies on the flexibility of factors such as wages and prices to combat underemployment.

Piacentini's study (2018) engages the exogenous-injection logic of the Keynesian multiplier at the level of theory and policy argument rather than through a fresh econometric test. The paper sets this demand-side, injection-based logic against the supply-side, NAIRU-inspired framework that has been prominent in European labor-market policy since the 1980s, where unemployment is blamed on labor-market institutions and incentives rather than by any exogenous spending shortcomings. Piacentini insists that the exogenous-injection logic of the Kahn–Keynes multiplier is not an outdated theoretical curiosity but an alternative to supply-side accounts that dispense with the very idea of an autonomous demand shock driving employment.

Fabrizio Carmignani's Griffith University discussion paper gives a deeper understanding as to whether an increase in government expenditure acts as an exogenous injection that multiplies into higher output and employment as suggested by the Kahn–Keynes framework. In his study conducted in Australia, Carmignani's central methodological task is to isolate the exogenous component of government spending from its endogenous response to the economy. Under the assumption that government consumption doesn't respond immediately to shocks in employment, the portion of movement in government spending uncorrelated with same-quarter movements in output and employment can be treated as exogenous, in the similar and specific sense the Kahn–Keynes model requires. The study concludes with the main findings. First, the output multiplier associated with the exogenous spending shock exceeds one. Second, employment responds positively, though the strength of the result depends on the measure used: full-time employment responds with reasonable precision, while total employment, which includes part-time work, is positive on average but too imprecisely estimated to distinguish from zero.

A similar study was conducted by Bebonchu Atems (2019) using the same identification problem as Carmignani albeit in the United States rather than a national economy. The paper's central finding is that exogenous state government spending shocks have so-called "Keynesian effects" referring to a positive innovation to government spending that raises not only output but also employment and the real wage, precisely the joint pattern the Kahn–Keynes mechanism predicts. New employment financed by the injection and rising real incomes as additional workers are drawn into production. Atems's more distinctive contribution, however, is to show that this exogenous-injection multiplier is far from constant. It is larger during recessions than expansions, larger when public debt is low than when it is high and varies with the stringency of a state's balanced-budget rules and debt-restriction provisions, with the sectoral composition of the economy.

2.3

From Investment Injection to Employment: A Three-Stage Mechanism

Public infrastructure investment can affect economic outcomes influenced by more than the initial injection of government spending since its effects depend also on the infrastructure produced from that spending and the economic activity linked with it. Infrastructure has been long associated with economic development, specifically in developing economies wherein gaps in physical infrastructure can limit productive activity. For example, Han et al. (2020) examined infrastructure investment in relation to output per worker and made emphasis on the role of infrastructure in supporting economic development. The study views infrastructure investment in terms of the resources committed, the infrastructure produced from those sources and the resulting effects on economic activity. These distinctions can be understood by the lens of investment output (financial resources that were committed to the infrastructure), physical output (infrastructure produced by that spending) and production (economic output). It is also relevant to note that employment may subsequently be considered as a labor market outcome that is concerned with these economic effects.

Investment output→Physical output→Production→Employment

The first distinction concerns investment output which refers to the recorded financial value dedicated to the infrastructure before that spending has necessarily translated into usable physical assets. According to the IMF (2015), public investment is measured as general government gross fixed capital formation which includes the total net value of government acquisitions of fixed assets during a given accounting period. The financial resources dedicated to public investment then represent an input into infrastructure provision instead of the infrastructure itself and it is important to know the difference between the amount spent and what is ultimately produced because the efficiency of public investment concerns how effective these financial resources are when converted into public capital and infrastructure outcomes.

At the second stage, the investment output must be then converted into actual physical output before wider economic effects can take place. Public infrastructure refers to the network of physical assets made by public investment which includes economic infrastructure like highways, roads, railways, airports, water and sewer systems and utilities as well social infrastructure like public schools and hospitals (IMF, 2015). However, the spending on these projects does not automatically mean that the corresponding infrastructure has been completed or is already providing services. Herrera et al. (2025) found that developing countries show lower efficiency in infrastructure spending than in human capital spending while the findings also point out the need for improvements in project evaluation and execution and further emphasized that better project selection and management in infrastructure are central to physical capital formation. As a result, recorded investment spending may not fully correspond to the amount of physical infrastructure actually delivered.

At the third stage, physical infrastructure can affect production by improving the conditions under which firms and workers operate and by supporting productivity and economic activity. Han et al. (2020) note that infrastructure can generate greater competition and increase firm productivity while stagnant infrastructure investment has also been linked with slower productivity growth. Ramey (2020) reviewed the macroeconomic effects of infrastructure investment and notes that the long run output effects can be sizable when government capital is productive. Findings from their study provide a basis for linking physical infrastructure to broader economic output although it is important to consider that the magnitude of the relationship can vary across infrastructure types and economic settings.

Evidence from a broader infrastructure research further shows that infrastructure can be associated with labor market outcomes alongside its effects on production and productivity. Foster et al. (2023) examined more than 1,000 estimates from 221 studies covering transport, energy, and digital infrastructure and a range of outcomes, including output, productivity, employment, and wages. The results of their analysis had particularly strong effects of transport infrastructure on labor market outcomes, their analysis of developing country samples also finds larger estimated elasticities than developed economies for transport and digital infrastructure, though this pattern does not hold for energy infrastructure. Such differences found across sectors and contexts indicate that infrastructure may extend beyond production and productivity to employment and other labor market outcomes.

Aside from this, employment effects can also arise directly during the implementation of infrastructure investment. Moszoro (2021) used firm level panel data covering 41 countries over 19 years, estimated the direct employment effects of public investment in infrastructure which includes electricity, roads, schools and hospitals as well as water and sanitation. The results indicate that 1 million USD in infrastructure spending is linked to approximately 3-7 jobs in advanced economies, 10-17 jobs in emerging markets, and 16-30 jobs in low income developing countries. Although these estimates are concerned with direct employment effects rather than the overall regional employment, they provide evidence that infrastructure investment can generate employment during the implementation of infrastructure projects.

2.4

Econometric Justification for Three-Stage Least Squares (3SLS)

Economic relationships usually involve variables that influence one another simultaneously rather than operating in a single manner. Muslim et al. (2022) explained that an endogenous variable in one equation may appear as an explanatory variable in another which creates an interdependence within the system and the possibility of endogeneity when these variables are correlated with the error terms. Mourad and Trabulsi (2019) also emphasize that structural equations should be specified according to the underlying economic theory with endogenous and exogenous variables appropriately distinguished before the estimations.

The presence of endogenous variables creates limitations for ordinary least squares (OLS). When an explanatory variable is correlated with the error term, the OLS estimator may become biased and inconsistent which makes it inappropriate for estimating the structural relationships. Alam et al. (2019) note in their study of broadband penetration and economic growth using a panel simultaneous equation framework, stating that endogeneity causes inconsistency in usual OLS estimates and thus requiring instrumental variable methods such as two-stage least squares (2SLS) to get consistent parameter estimates.

Two-stage least squares provide an instrumental-variable approach for estimating individual structural equations in the presence of an endogenous explanatory variable. Zellner and Theil (1962) describe 2SLS as a method in which the jointly dependent variables are first estimated using the information from the reduced form before estimating the coefficients of a structural equation. For the systems that contain several interconnected structural equations, the three-stage least squares (3SLS) extend the two-stage approach by incorporating information from the system as a whole.

Zellner and Theil (1962) developed the 3SLS as a method that uses two-stage least squares estimated moment matrix of the structural disturbances to estimate the coefficients of the entire system simultaneously. The method therefore combines the instrumental-variables approach of 2SLS with information on the covariance among the disturbances of the structural equations. When structural disturbances across equations have nonzero contemporaneous covariances, 3SLS can provide an asymptotic efficiency gain relative to 2SLS. Zellner and Theil (1962) further explained that this efficiency gain depends on the covariance structure of the disturbances and when the disturbance covariance matrix is diagonal, 2SLS and 3SLS become equivalent while the nonzero cross equation allow 3SLS to make use of additional information from the system.

The application of simultaneous-equation methods to economic relationships also shows the relevance of this approach to models involving interconnected economic outcomes. For example, Alam et al. (2019) jointly estimated equations involving broadband penetration and economic growth while considering the covariance between the error terms across equations. In their study, it was noted that while 2SLS addresses the endogeneity of individual structural equations, it does not account for the covariance between the errors of different equations which then leads the study to consider 3SLS and its GMM variant.

Onakoya et al. (2012) used a multivariate simultaneous-equation model and 3SLS to investigate the relationship between infrastructure and economic growth in Nigeria. They explained that simultaneous-equation regression is particularly relevant when an endogenous variable in one equation appears as an explanatory variable in another which creates feedback between variables. Aside from this, they further used 3SLS to capture the transmission channels through which infrastructure affects economic growth. Additionally, their model considered infrastructure as both a direct input and an intermediate input in the production process and emphasized the possibility of inter-sectoral linkages through which infrastructure investment can influence broader economic outcomes.

2.5

The Implementation Bottlenecks and the Philippine Context

In the Philippine context, encompassing the years 2016 to 2023, the study captures the timeframe of a critical transition in Philippine Fiscal policy. This scopes the administration of Duterte's Build, Build, Build (BBB) Program and the transition to Marcos's administration, which revitalized the BBB program into the Build Better More (BBM) program, which faces a lot of criticism with the ongoing issues regarding flood control projects. Hence, this issue made this study relevant and helpful, as the Public Infrastructure obligation and the Fiscal Policy shift over time. Inadequate public infrastructure has been a major constraint to economic growth in the Philippines. Addressing the following constraints, the Philippine Development Plan (PDP) 2023-2028 built a medium term fiscal framework which targets public infrastructure spending ranging from 5% to 6% of the Gross Domestic Product (BSP, 2025). Furthermore, the World Bank and Asian Development Bank (ADB) have identified through their rigorous studies that poor transport networks and logistics act as a critical constraint to private investment (World Bank, 2020). For the country to recover this deficit, the Philippine Government initiated a massive fiscal push during the "Build, Build, Build" program from 2017 to 2022, which raised infrastructure spending from an average of 2.6% in 2013 to 2016 to 4.6% of the GDP (DepDev, 2023). Consequently, the current administration's program "Build Better More" (BBM) seeks to sustain the current momentum by maintaining the PDP medium-term infrastructure spending framework of 5% to 6% of the GDP. Moreover, as early as 2024, Build Better More programs encompass 200 infrastructure projects, amounting to approximately USD 176.7 billion (BSP, 2025). Notably, approximately 70% of the active Infrastructure projects under the Build Better, More agenda were carried over from the Build, Build, Build programs, demonstrating the long-term nature of multiple administrations' commitment to these capital investments (Chanco, 2026).

Furthermore, local macroeconomic evaluations have shown that the pace of public investment momentum cannot easily be sustained due to government agencies suffering from severe "absorptive capacity" constraints, which the countries implementing increasing infrastructure programs are generally taken for granted by policymakers. According to the study of Patalinghug (2020), this also includes the issue of policymakers selecting infrastructure programs with high expected rates of return, which the policymakers may not have the capacity to execute. The study of Patalinghug (2019) published by the Stratbase Institute which notes that when public investment exceeds a certain threshold, the country lacks the institutional, technical, and managerial skills to efficiently transform those investments into sustained output and employment growth. Consequently, the official cost of investment is often disconnected from its effective value due to governmental inefficiencies.

In addition, an analysis by the Philippine Institute for Development Studies (PIDS) (2022), reveals a systematic lag in how infrastructure agencies absorb and spend their budgets. In simple terms, data from the Department of Budget and Management (DBM) shows that while the Department of Public Works and Highways (DPWH) achieved an obligation rate of 92.5% in the year 2020, where the actual disbursement rate relative to the obligation rate was a mere 31.2%. Other studies from PIDS evaluations come up with a similar low DPWH disbursement rate of 45.8% during the same period. This shows the possibility that there is a massive infrastructure budgets obligated between 2016 and 2023 that largely sat in a prolonged process of planning and stalled construction phases (Navarro and Latigar, 2022). In the employment perspective, localized hiring only occurs when funds are actually disbursed to contractors and workers. Consequently, according to the PIDS study, crippling delays between budget obligation and cash disbursement are further intensified by systemic implementation bottlenecks. A study also identified some constraints that affected the execution of the infrastructure, which may have resulted from protracted Right of Way (ROW) acquisition, legal disputes, natural calamities, and poor project preparation as persistent barriers to transport infrastructure execution (Navarro and Latigar, 2022).

From an employment perspective, the Philippine economy has demonstrated dynamic and sustained growth over the past decades; furthermore, this expansion has not been relevant to poverty reduction and high-quality job creation, leading to an economic phenomenon often described as "jobless growth" (World Bank, 2022; Paqueo et al., 2014). Consequently, neighboring East Asian economies successfully transitioned from agricultural to labor-intensive manufacturing, while the Philippines largely overlooked industrialization and moved directly from an agricultural base into a highly polarized service sector (World Bank, 2022). While the economy has generated millions of jobs and unemployment fell by 5% in recent years, the structural reality of the country's labor market is characterized by pervasive underemployment, which averaged nearly 20% of the total employment from 2010 to 2017 and ranged between 12.3% to 15.2% in recent years. Economic expansions tend to idle labor into the workforce, but often through informal work, low-skilled, and part-time arrangements rather than high-quality wage jobs.

2.6

Synthesis and Applicability of the Reviewed Literature

The literature reviewed throughout this chapter revolves on the common underlying premise of public infrastructure investment functions as an exogenous demand injection capable of generating measurable, though highly conditional, effects on output and employment. Cross-country evidence from advanced economies (Abiad et al., 2016) and from a broader panel of emerging and developing economies (Adarov et al., 2026) both confirm that unanticipated public investment shocks produce positive, statistically significant multipliers on GDP, with the effect magnified in economies where fiscal space, government efficiency, and capital scarcity are present. This macro-level finding is reinforced at the subnational level in the Philippine setting by Yang (2026), whose regional analysis shows that infrastructure-poor regions capture substantially larger multiplier effects than infrastructure-dense regions such as Metro Manila which can be connected to a pattern that grounds the present study's expectation that the employment impact of public spending should not be treated as spatially uniform across the country.

The reviewed literature is also consistent in informing that the strength of this relationship is not automatic. The Keynesian Multiplier and Kahn's Employment Mechanism (Piacentini, 2018; Carmignani, 2014; Atems, 2019) strengthens the theoretical background connecting exogenous spending injections to output and employment, but the empirical work built on this foundation (Han et al., 2020; Foster et al., 2023; Moszoro, 2021) shows that the transitions from spending to jobs passes through different stages and that the strength of the relationship can weaken or stall at any given moment. This can be directly relevant to the Philippine case, where Patalinghug (2020) and Navarro and Latigar (2022) document a persistent execution gap wherein high budget obligation rates alongside low disbursement rates, driven by factors such as right-of-way disputes, procurement issues, and weak absorptive capacity within responsible government agencies. These findings may imply that measuring infrastructure's employment effect purely through budgetary allocation risks inaccuracy towards actual labor market impact if execution issues are not accounted for.

Methodologically, the studies reviewed on simultaneous-equation estimation solidifies the paper's decision to use 3SLS rather than a single-equation approach (Zellner & Theil, 1962; Onakoya et al., 2012; Alam et al., 2019; Muslim et al., 2022). A 3SLS framework allows the interdependence between the stages between investment, physical infrastructure output, and employment to be modeled explicitly and allows for efficiency gains over 2SLS when disruptions across equations are correlated, which prior applications to infrastructure-growth relationships (Onakoya et al., 2012) suggest is likely to happen.

Overall, the literature supports this paper's central argument that the employment effect of Philippine public infrastructure investment cannot be adequately captured by spending figures alone. Instead, it must be traced through the sequential and interdependent stages of investment, physical completion, and production, a sequence shaped by the country's documented institutional and implementation constraints.
